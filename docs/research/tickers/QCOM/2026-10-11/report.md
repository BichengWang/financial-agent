## Business

Device manufacturers buy Qualcomm chips for high-performance, low-power compute and connectivity; licensed manufacturers separately pay royalties for patented cellular technology whether or not they buy every Qualcomm chip. Repeat demand comes from new devices and standards, not a single subscription. Alternatives include MediaTek, OEM internal silicon and rival platforms, but patented standards can still carry licensing obligations [issuer-annual].

The strongest bear case accepts patent profits but rejects the price if handset content declines faster than new platform earnings arrive. Legal tails are not smooth-growth shocks and cannot be hidden in a discount-rate penalty. Avoid anchoring on either tax-depressed GAAP EPS or fully adjusted earnings.

## Industry

Qualcomm occupies fabless compute/connectivity and cellular-IP layers. Smartphone units may mature while compute content grows; edge AI and automotive could expand demand, but competing OEM silicon and software ecosystems constrain capture. Licensing depends on device volumes and valid patents, not solely Qualcomm chip share.

Limited third-party suppliers and China/customer concentration connect geopolitics to both costs and demand. Automotive design cycles differ from phones; PCs, robotics and data centers have different incumbents. No independently sourced numerical TAM is retained. Management’s FY2029 targets are not analyst EPS forecasts or assumed market share [issuer-annual], [issuer-update].

## Decision

Watchlist: attractive licensing economics and actual diversification, but price lacks sufficient cushion under base/probability-weighted discounted assumptions. Business quality is separate from valuation confidence. Review license capture, ex-Apple handset content, auto/IoT margins, Modular/new-market returns, compensation and actual shares. Thesis breaks if royalties materially erode or new platform profits fail to replace declining premium content.

Duan's business/people lens separates customer usefulness from stewardship; Buffett's durability lens asks whether the franchise and cash survive a market closure; Munger's inversion emphasizes specific failure mechanisms; Li Lu's structural lens asks who captures technology gains.
